Who Is the Seminar For?

The seminar is open to Master’s students at the WFI (e.g., in the BAOR program) and to students in the Master’s program in Data Science.

You should have completed at least one Master’s-level statistics course (e.g., Predictive Analytics) or hold a Bachelor’s degree with a quantitative focus. Most importantly, you should be curious about how and why statistical methods work, not just how to apply them.

“Learn from me, if not by my precepts, at least by my example, how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge.”

— Victor Frankenstein, student at Ingolstadt
Mary Shelley, Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus (1818), Vol. I, Ch. IV

Victor Frankenstein did not fail because his creation was weak. He failed because he brought it to life without fully understanding it, and then walked away from it.

Statistical methods are no different. Software makes it easy to stitch together an estimator, a test, and a p-value, and to throw the switch. But a method applied without understanding its assumptions, its mechanics, and its failure modes will sooner or later turn against its user.

Become knowledgeable users of statistical methods.
Otherwise, they become your monster.

In this seminar, you take one statistical method into the lab: you dissect it, bring it to life in code, and then deliberately try to make it fail using Monte Carlo simulations. You leave the lab as its master, not its victim.

Lab Protocol

  1. Equip the lab. We start together: in the first meetings, we learn how to build Monte Carlo simulations and use them to make statistical methods fail (1  Monte Carlo Simulations: The Laboratory).
  2. Assign the specimens. Together, we allocate the topics, one statistical method per lab group (2  Topics: The Specimens).
  3. Dissect it. Work out its assumptions, its mechanics, and how it turns on its users, and write it all down in your seminar thesis (3  Writing the Seminar Thesis).
  4. Present it. Show your creature to the seminar, including the moment it turns on you.

Lab Rules

  • You work in lab groups of three.
  • Grading:
    • 80% seminar thesis, your written lab report
    • 20% presentation, where every group member speaks. Victor abandoned his creature; you stand by yours.
